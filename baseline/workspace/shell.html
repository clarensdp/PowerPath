An end-to-end playbook for developers and infrastructure investors.

What PowerPath does

PowerPath screens potential energy and data-center locations, organizes the steps needed to obtain usable power, compares grid and onsite supply alternatives, and underwrites the financial consequences of infrastructure choices and delays. It is not a utility capacity reservation, permit determination, engineering study or investment recommendation.

1. Overview — what needs my attention?

Choose an active project or create a new one. Check first usable power, capacity, critical-path warnings and missing evidence. Click a project to work its next action.

2. Find · Site map — where might I locate?

Compare state screening estimates for timing, modeled power cost, carbon and break-even rent. Select a location and inspect grid context, generation candidates, queue indicators and policy constraints. State-level screening is not site-specific capacity.

3. Find · Energy opportunities — can existing energy help?

Investigate existing plants, surplus/curtailed generation, brownfields or stranded gas. Confirm dependable MW, contractable rights, fiber, cooling and feasible timing; demo opportunities are fictional.

4. Plan · Connection plan — what must happen?

Enter required MW, jurisdiction, target dates, dependencies, permit tasks and accountable owners. Record dated evidence and document blockers. Calculated task dates are planning estimates until confirmed by the utility or relevant authority.

5. Underwrite · Power strategies — what can actually work?

Compare grid-only, onsite engines and hybrid supply under the same project assumptions. Set grid rights, air permits, firm gas supply, equipment evidence, carbon ceiling, reliability limit and deadline. A conditional strategy is not an executable recommendation.

6. Underwrite · Project model — what does it earn?

Set IT load, PUE, building opening share, occupancy, rent, campus costs, equipment and connection timing. Examine hourly and annual power mix, unserved demand, operating cash flow, NPV with and without terminal value, and downside sensitivities.

7. Data & method — can I trust the inputs?

Inspect calibration, sources, assumptions and model limitations. Hourly regional emissions and renewable shapes have public-data inputs; state power prices still use scaled ERCOT price patterns. Verify any project-specific input externally.

8. Export — what is decision-ready?

Save the case as JSON, export project task data, or print the IC brief. State whether the results are screened, conditional or supported by dated project evidence; include terminal value and relevant sensitivities.

Worked example · 150 MW campus

Step A: Start a new site-first project in Connection plan and enter 150 MW required capacity. Step B: choose a state on Site map and link the project. Step C: update first and full power targets, utility studies, air approvals and procurement lead times with evidence. Step D: in Project model, set IT MW = required facility MW ÷ PUE; compare grid-only / onsite / hybrid and adjust the evidence gates. Step E: review cost, carbon, unmet load, finance and downside results before exporting. The illustrative defaults are not a forecast for a specific 150 MW location.

How profitability is calculated

Monthly rental revenue = billable facility MWh ÷ 730 hours ÷ PUE × 1,000 × occupancy × $/IT kW-month. Billable workload includes relocated work and excludes interrupted work. Occupancy is an incremental commercial realization factor; reserved power costs are held fixed.

Cash flow = revenue − energy cash costs − non-energy opex − modeled interruption consequence − capex + terminal proceeds. NPV discounts month-end cash flow over the same 120-month development horizon. No debt, inflation, construction interest, or replacement capex is included; tax, depreciation, incentives and exit value come from the valuation layer described below.

The original benchmark annualizes energy equipment capex. This overlay removes that capital-recovery charge, retains fixed O&M, and charges actual modeled onsite equipment capex at its commitment month. Campus capex is additional and spread evenly over the original pre-opening construction months. This avoids charging energy capex twice. Interruption consequences are an economic reserve, not a verified invoice; interrupted workload also earns no rent.

Terminal proceeds default to zero. Campus capex excludes modeled onsite energy equipment and must include other owner-funded infrastructure. PUE only converts facility load into rentable IT capacity; it never increases the energy demand already modeled.
